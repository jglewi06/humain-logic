# Will AI take my job? An honest look, job by job

If you've typed "will AI take my job" into a search bar, you're far from alone, and it's a fair thing to wonder. Headlines swing between "AI will replace everyone" and "nothing will change." Neither is very useful when you're trying to plan your next few years.

This page is our calm, plain-language answer. Our job tracker covers **31 common jobs**, from data entry and customer service to nursing, plumbing and law. For each one we show two different things, with the sources behind them, and practical steps you can take today.

## The short answer

For most people, AI is more likely to **change your job** than to erase it any time soon. It is getting good at certain *tasks*, like drafting text, sorting data or answering routine questions. Most jobs are a bundle of many tasks, and plenty of those still need a person: judgment, hands-on work, trust, relationships and accountability.

Some jobs feel more pressure than others, though. Roles built mostly around routine computer work, such as data entry, are under real pressure now. Hands-on and caring jobs, such as nursing, electrical work and plumbing, are expected to keep growing.

## How our job tracker works

Every job in the tracker shows two separate estimates, because they answer two different questions.

**1. Hiring impact by 2030.** This is the near-term question: will there be fewer openings for this job in the next few years? We rate it as high, medium, low or growth, based on published research such as the [World Economic Forum's Future of Jobs Report 2025](https://www.weforum.org/publications/the-future-of-jobs-report-2025/) and [U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics projections](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/).

**2. Automation timeline.** This is our rough guess at when technology *could* handle most of a job's tasks without a person. It is a range of years, not a prediction about your specific workplace.

We want to be upfront: **the year ranges are HumAIn Logic editorial estimates, not figures published by the researchers we cite.** None of those sources gives a "this job ends in year X" date. We build the ranges from published research, including the [Frey & Osborne (2013) study of computerisation](https://www.oxfordmartin.ox.ac.uk/publications/the-future-of-employment) and [Microsoft Research's 2025 study of how generative AI applies to occupations](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/working-with-ai-measuring-the-occupational-implications-of-generative-ai/), and we label how confident we are. You can read the details on our [methodology page](/methodology).

## What the tracker shows right now

Here is a snapshot of where things stand in our data (last updated October 15, 2026).

**Jobs facing the most pressure ("shift")**: data entry keyers, general office clerks, bookkeeping clerks, telemarketers, customer service reps, bank tellers, cashiers, receptionists, payroll clerks, insurance claims processors, translators and paralegals. For example, employers surveyed by the WEF expect data entry clerk jobs to shrink about 34% between 2025 and 2030, mostly through fewer new hires ([WEF Future of Jobs Report 2025](https://reports.weforum.org/docs/WEF_Future_of_Jobs_Report_2025.pdf)).

**Jobs to watch ("watch")**: accountants and auditors, truck and delivery drivers, warehouse workers, graphic designers, writers, market research analysts, software developers and financial advisors. These jobs are changing, but a full takeover is a long way off.

**Steadier jobs ("steady")**: lawyers, K-12 teachers, registered nurses, electricians, plumbers, construction laborers, home health aides and physicians. Several of these are expected to grow by 2030.

## Why "tasks, not jobs" matters

It helps to think about your job as a list of tasks. Ask yourself:

- Which parts of my week are repetitive and done on a computer?
- Which parts need my judgment, my hands, or a relationship with someone?
- Which parts would I actually be glad to hand off?

The repetitive, screen-based tasks are the ones AI is most likely to speed up or take over. The rest is where your value grows. A paralegal who uses AI to draft a first summary still checks it, knows the client and spots what's missing. That checking and knowing is the job.

## Who's feeling it first

Research suggests early pressure lands hardest on entry-level, routine work rather than experienced workers. Stanford's Digital Economy Lab, for example, reports that experienced lawyers show little job loss so far, while pressure falls more on junior research and document work ([Brynjolfsson, Chandar & Chen, Aug 2026](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/app/uploads/2026/08/Canaries_August2026.pdf)). If you're early in your career, it's worth building skills AI can't easily copy, and learning to use AI tools well yourself.

## What you can do this week

You don't need to become a programmer. Small, steady steps go a long way:

1. **Try one AI tool on a boring task.** Use a free assistant to draft an email or summarize a long document, then check its work. Our [AI tool directory](/tools) lists free options.
2. **Take one free course.** [Google AI Essentials](https://grow.google/ai-essentials/) and [AI For Everyone on Coursera](https://www.coursera.org/learn/ai-for-everyone) (free to audit) are written for non-technical people.
3. **Write down your "human" tasks.** These are your talking points in reviews and interviews.
4. **Look at adjacent roles.** [O*NET OnLine](https://www.onetonline.org/) shows jobs that use similar skills.

Our free guide [AI and your job](/guides/ai-and-your-job) walks through this in more detail, and the [career action plan PDF](/bookstore) adds a self-check worksheet and resume tips.

## A note on honesty

Nobody knows exactly how this will play out, including us. Forecasts about technology are often wrong in both directions. We'd rather tell you that plainly than pretend to certainty. What we can promise is that every claim on our job pages links to its source, our estimates are labeled as estimates, and we update the data as new research arrives.

## Frequently asked questions

**Will AI take my job?**
For most jobs, AI is more likely to change the work than replace it soon. Routine, screen-based roles face the most near-term pressure. Hands-on and caring jobs are expected to grow. Check your job in our [tracker](/will-ai-take-my-job).

**Which jobs are safest from AI?**
In our data, jobs like registered nurse, electrician, plumber, teacher, home health aide and physician are rated "steady" with expected growth by 2030, because they rely on hands-on work, judgment and human trust. For example, the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics projects registered nurse employment to grow 6% from 2025 to 2035, with about 180,800 openings a year ([BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/healthcare/registered-nurses.htm)).

**Which jobs are most at risk from AI?**
Routine computer-based roles, like data entry, office clerks, telemarketers and bookkeeping clerks, face the most pressure, mostly through fewer new hires rather than sudden layoffs.

**Where do your year estimates come from?**
They are HumAIn Logic editorial estimates built from published research (Frey & Osborne, WEF, BLS, Microsoft Research). The cited researchers do not publish per-job "automation dates." See our [methodology](/methodology).

**What should I do if my job is on the "shift" list?**
Start small: learn one AI tool, take a free course, and note the parts of your job that need a person. Each job page lists specific next steps and free resources.
